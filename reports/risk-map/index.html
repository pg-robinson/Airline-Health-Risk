Airline financial resilience · 25 October 2026

Who has room to absorb the next shock?

Fuel costs rise. Ticket sales disappoint. Cash falls. Which airlines can pay their bills until trading recovers?

An early comparison, not a prediction of failure. We cannot yet establish the direction for 20 airlines. ANA and Emirates need more debt information before we can place them on the chart.

Borrowing buys time. Does it buy enough?

Higher fuel bills and disappointing ticket sales can eat through cash together. A backup loan may cover the gap, but it adds interest and must eventually be repaid. The danger comes when cash is falling and the airline cannot obtain the next money it needs.

Healthy: the business pays its bills and has room for a poor year. Under pressure: it needs savings or extra funding. In danger: a cash or loan limit is approaching without a credible way to close the gap.

Our colours show warning signs we have found. They do not yet prove how long each airline can keep paying. There is no single gearing number that makes every airline unrecoverable.

A simple example—not an airline forecast

An airline expects $500m of spare cash after its ordinary payments. Extra fuel costs take $300m. Disappointing ticket sales take another $588m. It is $388m short. If new borrowing costs 10% for a full year, it needs about $431m to cover both the gap and the new interest. A $400m credit line leaves about $28m still to find.

Assumptions: no fees, no repayment on the new loan that year, no cost savings, and the whole loan drawn for the year. The ticket-sales example assumes $10bn planned passenger revenue and seat occupancy of 80% rather than 85%, at unchanged capacity and fare mix.
