# Which airlines can survive a bad year without asking for rescue money?

Investor presentation draft · 26 October 2026. Financial evidence retains its original dates; this is not a fresh October cash estimate.

An airline can survive expensive fuel. It can survive a disappointing summer. The danger is that both happen while large loan repayments are coming due.

The flights cost more to operate, but passengers will not necessarily pay more. Raising fares may lose customers; discounting may fill the aircraft without making the flight profitable. Cash falls. The airline may borrow to cover the shortfall, if its loan conditions still allow it. That brings another interest bill, and eventually another repayment.

**The question is whether the business recovers before the money runs out—or before lenders stop making money available.**

## The story in six steps

1. **Fuel becomes more expensive.** The extra bill depends on the price of jet fuel, the amount used and any price protection already bought. A crude-oil price change is not an identical change in the airline's fuel bill.
2. **Ticket sales disappoint.** Some seats go empty, or the airline cuts fares to fill them. Wages, aircraft payments and many airport costs do not fall at the same speed. We test demand separately: an oil shock does not mechanically cause a specified fall in traffic.
3. **Cash comes in below plan.** The airline still has to pay staff, suppliers, aircraft leases, interest and maturing loans. A profit figure alone does not tell us whether those payments can be met.
4. **The airline uses its backup funding.** Drawing an agreed credit line buys time only if it is still available. Borrowing increases cash and debt at the outset; it does not repair operating losses. As the cash is spent, the additional debt remains.
5. **The next year becomes harder.** New interest takes more money out of the business. Assets already promised to lenders cannot freely support another loan. Lenders may demand more security or restrict access to cash.
6. **The recovery arrives—or outside help becomes necessary.** If trading cannot cover costs and repayments before usable funding is exhausted, the airline must cut commitments, sell assets, raise equity, renegotiate debt or restructure.

## A simple example: borrowing buys time, but costs money

**Invented teaching example, not JetBlue or any other airline. All amounts are USD millions over one year.**

Suppose an airline expects $500m of spare cash after ordinary bills, necessary aircraft spending and scheduled debt payments. It expects an 85% seat occupancy rate. Fuel becomes more expensive, adding $300m after price protection and fare recovery. Occupancy reaches only 80%, with the same seats flown, distance mix and fare per passenger.

If planned passenger revenue was $10,000m, actual passenger revenue would be approximately $9,412m: $10,000m × 80/85. That is a $588m shortfall—not a 5% revenue decline. For simplicity, assume the missed revenue becomes an equal cash shortfall, with no cost savings or timing effects.

| What changes? | Cash effect |
|---|---:|
| Spare cash expected before the shock | +$500m |
| Extra fuel bill | −$300m |
| Lower ticket sales | −$588m |
| Shortfall before new borrowing costs | **−$388m** |

Assume the airline can borrow at 10%, with no fees or principal repayments on that new borrowing during the year. To cover both the $388m shortfall and a full year's interest on the new loan, it needs about **$431m**, not $388m: $388m ÷ 90%. Roughly $43m of the new loan pays its own first-year interest. Drawing later would change that calculation.

If only $400m can still be drawn, that loan provides $360m after a full year's interest. The airline still needs about **$28m** from existing cash, savings or another source. It has not automatically failed; we must compare the gap with its usable cash and the timing of payments.

**This is the feedback loop we want to measure for each airline: weaker trading → cash shortfall → more borrowing → higher bills → less time to recover.** We must not count the same loss once through lower fares and again through lower total revenue. Where fuel costs, capacity and demand move together, their assumptions must be consistent.

## Where is the breaking point?

There is no gearing number that makes every airline unrecoverable. An airline with more debt but reliable profits and distant repayments can have more time than one with less debt, falling cash and a repayment next month. Negative equity also makes debt/equity ratios misleading.

Instead, ask four concrete questions:

| Question for management | Warning sign |
|---|---|
| How much cash can you actually spend, and what must remain to keep flying? | The lowest monthly cash balance approaches the operating reserve |
| What can you still borrow under today's agreements? | The cash shortfall exceeds facilities that remain drawable under stress |
| When must the money be paid back? | A large repayment arrives before the recovery or replacement financing |
| Which loan condition would be hit first? | A specific cash, earnings or asset-security test restricts funding or accelerates repayment |

A planning cash reserve is a judgement. A loan condition is a contract. A credit-rating warning is an opinion. We report them separately. Hitting one does not automatically mean the airline has defaulted; remedies and lender decisions matter.

For each scenario, the useful limit is: **the largest combined hit to fuel costs and ticket sales the airline can absorb before a specified cash or loan limit is reached.** If we cannot verify that limit, we say so. We do not replace it with a made-up danger score.

## JetBlue: can the turnaround happen before it needs more money?

JetBlue's problem is that it enters the next shock after a long period of losses. Its reported annual net losses for 2020–2025 total $3.605bn; the first half of 2026 adds $566m. That history shows why a recovery matters. It does not mean the company burned the same amount of cash. [Loss history and annual source references](credit-story.md).

At June 2026 it reported about $2.2bn of unrestricted liquidity and a separate $600m undrawn credit line. Those are dated figures, not today's cash or a guarantee that the facility can be drawn in every scenario. [June filing, liquidity discussion](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1158463/000115846326000075/jblu-20260630.htm).

Our existing illustration starts with $2.168bn of cash and investments and keeps $1bn aside as an author-chosen operating reserve. That leaves $1.168bn to absorb ordinary cash needs and shocks before new funding. In a deliberately difficult combination of higher fuel, weaker revenue, higher rates and cash held back by a payment processor, the shocks consume about $1.043bn. Only **$125m** remains for ordinary cash consumption before reaching that chosen reserve. [Exact scenario assumptions and calculation](credit-story.md#what-combination-of-shocks-would-exhaust-the-buffer).

That is not a forecast of failure. It tells us what to investigate: would ordinary cash needs exceed $125m in that scenario, when would the lowest balance occur, and would backup borrowing still be available then? Any new borrowing must bring its own interest and fees into the calculation. The existing illustration does not yet solve those monthly funding decisions.

The original loyalty-financing agreement also contains cash and security tests that may redirect money towards lenders. These tests apply to that financing arrangement; they are not interchangeable with the airline's overall gearing. Current amendments and calculation statements still need checking. [Original agreement](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1158463/000115846324000023/exhibit41-jetbluextrueblue.htm).

**Investor conclusion:** JetBlue must show that trading is improving fast enough to reduce its need for more borrowing. A new loan alone proves it has obtained time, not that it has solved the problem. A deterioration in cash at the same time as reduced access to borrowing would be a much stronger warning than a high gearing ratio by itself.

## What does a healthy airline look like?

It can pay for normal operations, necessary aircraft spending and debt payments from the money its business produces over the cycle. It has enough spare cash and dependable backup funding to handle a poor year. It can repay or refinance loans without depending on an emergency sale or a sudden rebound in bookings.

On our present chart, Ryanair is a useful stronger starting point: less debt relative to its assets and a larger operating-profit cushion. JetBlue and Alaska have updated operating losses; easyJet and LATAM have falling margins but remain profitable on that measure. These are different situations. The [map and dated evidence](risk-map-guide.md) explain the differences. The chart does not yet establish who can fund every payment in a prolonged downturn.

## Seven ways the plan can go wrong

| Risk | Say it plainly | What we need to measure |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel | Each flight costs more, or fuel is harder to obtain | Extra cash cost after price protection and fare recovery |
| War and airspace closures | Flights are cancelled, rerouted or made longer | Lost sales, extra flying costs and compensation |
| Weaker demand | Fewer passengers buy tickets, or they pay less | Seats sold and fares compared with plan |
| Borrowing costs | Loans cost more, or replacement loans are unavailable | Added interest and repayments without committed funding |
| Currency | Dollar bills rise faster than local-currency ticket income | Unprotected difference between receipts and payments |
| Labour | Staff cost more, or too few qualified staff are available | Wage increases, cancelled flights and hiring/training needs |
| Engines and parts | Aircraft sit on the ground while bills keep arriving | Lost flying days, replacement aircraft costs and compensation |

An airline can face several at once. A disclosed risk is not proof of an actual shortage. Price protection may delay a fuel shock; it does not necessarily remove it. Missing evidence stays visible.

## The one-page story for every airline

**Starting point:** Is it making money, and is its cash position improving?

**The bad year:** What if fuel is dearer and ticket sales miss the plan?

**The bill:** How much extra cash is needed, and in which months?

**The backup:** How much can it still borrow, on what terms, and what interest does that add?

**The limit:** Which cash or loan condition is reached first, and how far away is it?

**The way out:** What specific improvement or action would close the gap?

Until the monthly cash forecasts, loan conditions and repayment schedules are complete, the last three answers remain conditional. That is where the next research effort belongs.
